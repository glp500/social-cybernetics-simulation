# Costly enforcement and polycentric governance

## Citations

- Fehr, E., & Gächter, S. (2002). Altruistic punishment in humans. *Nature, 415*, 137–140.
  <https://doi.org/10.1038/415137a>
- Dreber, A., Rand, D. G., Fudenberg, D., & Nowak, M. A. (2008). Winners don't punish. *Nature,
  452*, 348–351. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06723>
- Ostrom, E. (2010). Beyond markets and states: polycentric governance of complex economic
  systems. *American Economic Review, 100*, 641–672.
  <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.3.641>
- Ostrom, E. (2012). Nested externalities and polycentric institutions: must we wait for global
  solutions to climate change before taking actions at other scales? *Economic Theory, 49*,
  353–369. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-010-0558-6>

## Why this belongs

The research question concerns not just rule adoption but persistent bounded institutions and the
costly actions that enforce them. It also asks whether governance patterns become nested across
local, regional, and system scopes.

## Supporting evidence

Fehr and Gächter experimentally show that people may incur costs to punish defectors and that the
availability of punishment can sustain cooperation in their public-goods setting. Ostrom's work
supports analyzing diverse, complementary governance arrangements operating at multiple scales
rather than presuming a single market or state solution.

## Counterevidence and boundary conditions

Dreber et al. find that costly punishment can increase cooperation without increasing group payoff,
and that higher earners punish less in their repeated-game setting. Punishment can therefore be a
costly outcome rather than a welfare improvement. Ostrom's polycentric analysis does not imply that
independently diffused local, regional, and system labels constitute effective nested governance;
complementarity, coordination, boundaries, and outcomes must be demonstrated.

## Justified commitment

Enforcement should be an optional, costly principal action whose incidence and welfare effects are
measured separately from rule adoption. Multi-scale governance should be identified through raw
continuous evidence of persistent, bounded, and coordinated patterns rather than assumed from a
category label.

## Project choices, not literature calibration

- restricting enforcement to the enforcer's current cell;
- enforcement cost, sanction magnitude, detection, and targeting rules;
- the local/regional/system scope geometry and interaction between scopes;
- persistence thresholds and the operational definition of nesting;
- whether anti-social, mistaken, or discriminatory enforcement is permitted.

The baseline should retain both no-enforcement and cost-free diagnostic controls.

## Verification target

- [ ] enforcement conserves physical resources and debits the enforcer's declared cost
- [ ] adoption, attempted enforcement, successful enforcement, and welfare effects remain distinct
- [ ] scope boundaries and cross-scope interactions are present in raw records
- [ ] institutional labels never replace continuous outcome and topology measures
